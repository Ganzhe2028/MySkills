# Learning Method

## Operating model

Use a short, evidence-driven loop:

1. **Attempt**: the learner performs a bounded task.
2. **Observe**: answers, annotations, pauses, questions, and abandonment reveal the actual bottleneck.
3. **Diagnose**: classify the mechanism instead of judging the person.
4. **Practice**: isolate one or two mechanisms in a small worksheet.
5. **Retrieve**: recover the method or relationship without copying.
6. **Transfer**: apply the method to unfamiliar material.
7. **Fade**: reduce support only after repeated transfer.

This combines cognitive-load management, scaffolding, formative feedback, retrieval practice, deliberate practice, and transfer. Use the concepts to make decisions; do not turn the learner-facing page into a theory essay.

## Bottleneck taxonomy

Use one primary code and, only when necessary, one secondary code.

| Code | Meaning | Evidence examples | Typical response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| K | Knowledge gap | unknown word, grammar form, concept, or task rule | teach the minimum missing item, then retry |
| P | Processing load | words are familiar but the sentence cannot be integrated; attention drops in dense text | chunk material, reduce simultaneous operations |
| S | Strategy | searches by superficial keywords, misreads task type, or validates the wrong evidence | teach one decision rule and practise it |
| O | Output | understands the content but cannot express it clearly | constrained speaking or writing with feedback |
| E | Execution | spelling, answer format, timing, or accidental omission | add an execution checklist or timed repetition |
| F | Worksheet friction | cannot tell where to type, what to do next, or why an activity exists | redesign the worksheet before adding academic difficulty |

Attention comments are not automatically a stable attention problem. If attention drops during dense second-language processing, classify the observable event as `P` unless other evidence supports a different explanation.

## Attempt-state audit

Read an attempt in four layers when the evidence exists:

1. **First response**: what the learner did before checking or revising.
2. **Reason**: the rule, evidence, or operation used to justify that response.
3. **Self-correction**: what the learner changed before seeing the supplied answer, and what triggered the change.
4. **Final answer**: the response left at the end.

These layers answer different questions. A correct final answer with a wrong reason is not successful transfer. A self-correction made before answer reveal shows emerging control of the rule, but does not prove automaticity. Preserve both facts in the diagnosis.

The learner's description of where they struggled is evidence of experience, not a definitive localization of the mechanism. Compare it with the work trace. If the learner says “I was stuck on the signs” but every sign is correct and the interval selection is wrong, locate the bottleneck at the sign-to-interval conversion.

Find the earliest broken conversion:

1. write the task as a short chain of visible operations;
2. mark the last operation completed correctly;
3. mark the first operation that became wrong, missing, or unclear;
4. preserve or supply the earlier correct results in the next worksheet;
5. practise the one conversion between those two points.

Use completion time only as supporting evidence. Faster work matters only when accuracy, reasoning, task scope, and the amount of support are also known. Do not equate a shorter time on a narrower task with mastery.

## Selecting the next task

| Evidence | Next page | Default support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The learner stopped or misunderstood the format | Diagnostic | Full |
| The learner found a related sentence but it did not answer the question | Micro-practice on evidence validation | Full |
| Words are mostly known but a long sentence is not understood | Micro-practice on sentence skeleton and semantic chunks | Full or reduced |
| The learner solved familiar material after feedback | Transfer with new material | Reduced |
| Two consecutive transfer tasks succeed without instruction confusion | Broader task or lower scaffold | Reduced or independent |
| A new task type appears | Micro-practice | Full |

Choose the smallest task that can change the diagnosed mechanism. Do not assign a complete mock test merely to measure again.

## Retrieval and transfer

Retrieval must target something worth remembering:

- a decision rule;
- a sentence relationship;
- the sequence of a method;
- a useful expression;
- the reason evidence supports an answer.

Do not ask the learner to recall an arbitrary answer letter unless answer-format speed is the explicit skill.

Transfer must use new material. Repeating the same item after the answer has been shown measures memory of the answer, not transfer of the method.

## Feedback format

Keep learner-facing feedback concrete:

1. what the learner actually did;
2. what mechanism that reveals;
3. the smallest correction;
4. the next practice objective.

Quote or point to evidence. Avoid generic encouragement, trait claims, or an inflated score.

## Scaffold fading

Track the micro-skill, not overall confidence.

- Start a new micro-skill at Level 1 full support.
- After one successful familiar retry, keep the level and use new material.
- After two consecutive successful transfer attempts with no `F` evidence, remove one category of support.
- If transfer fails, restore the previous level and isolate the failure.
- If instructions cause friction, fix the worksheet before judging the academic response.

Remove only one category at a time: hints, sentence starters, chunk labels, or explicit sequencing. Keep the time limit and acceptance criteria at every level.

Curriculum progress and scaffold fading are separate decisions. A learner may advance after a correct new-material transfer that includes an explicit self-correction, while a short checkpoint remains in the next task. Lower the support level only under the repeated-transfer rule above.
